# Photosynthesis: How Plants Capture Light Energy

Photosynthesis is the fundamental process by which plants, algae, and certain bacteria convert
light energy into chemical energy stored as glucose. This process is the foundation of almost all
life on Earth, as it produces the oxygen we breathe and the organic compounds that form the base
of most food chains.

## The Light-Dependent Reactions

The first stage of photosynthesis occurs in the thylakoid membranes of the chloroplast. When
sunlight strikes chlorophyll and other pigment molecules, electrons are energised and passed along
the electron transport chain. This movement of electrons drives the synthesis of ATP and NADPH,
two energy-carrying molecules essential for the next stage.

During this stage, water molecules are split in a process called photolysis. Each water molecule
yields two hydrogen ions, two electrons, and one oxygen atom. The oxygen atoms combine to form O2,
which is released as a by-product — the oxygen that sustains aerobic life on Earth. The hydrogen
ions and electrons are passed to NADP+ to form NADPH.

Light energy is captured by two photosystems: Photosystem II absorbs light at 680 nm and
Photosystem I absorbs light at 700 nm. They work in tandem to drive the production of both ATP
and NADPH from ADP, NADP+, and the electrons extracted from water.

## The Calvin Cycle (Light-Independent Reactions)

The second stage takes place in the stroma of the chloroplast. Using the ATP and NADPH produced
in the light-dependent reactions, the Calvin cycle fixes atmospheric carbon dioxide into organic
molecules through a series of enzyme-catalysed steps.

Carbon dioxide is incorporated into a five-carbon molecule called ribulose bisphosphate (RuBP) by
the enzyme RuBisCO — the most abundant enzyme on Earth. The resulting six-carbon intermediate
immediately splits into two three-carbon molecules of 3-phosphoglycerate (3-PGA). ATP and NADPH
then reduce these molecules to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G3P), a versatile three-carbon sugar.

Most G3P is used to regenerate RuBP, keeping the cycle running. A smaller fraction is drawn off
to synthesise glucose, sucrose, starch, and other organic compounds that fuel the plant's growth
and metabolism. Three turns of the Calvin cycle are needed to produce one molecule of G3P net.

## Factors Affecting the Rate of Photosynthesis

Several environmental and internal factors regulate how quickly photosynthesis proceeds.

**Light intensity** is often the primary limiting factor. As light levels rise, the rate of
photosynthesis increases until another factor becomes limiting. Very high intensities can damage
the photosystems through photo-oxidation.

**Carbon dioxide concentration** limits the Calvin cycle. Modern atmospheric CO2 (~420 ppm) is
below the optimum for many crops, which is why greenhouses sometimes elevate CO2 levels to boost
yields.

**Temperature** affects enzyme activity. Photosynthesis peaks at an optimal temperature —
typically 25–35 °C for most plants — and declines sharply above or below that range as enzymes
denature or slow down.

**Water availability** matters because water is both a raw material (providing electrons via
photolysis) and a regulator of stomatal opening. Drought causes stomata to close, restricting CO2
entry and dramatically slowing the Calvin cycle.

Understanding these factors is critical for agriculture, ecology, and efforts to engineer more
efficient crops in a changing climate.
